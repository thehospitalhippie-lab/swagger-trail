Change Your Walk, Change Your Life: The One Exercise That Starts Everything

“Happiness is when what you think, what you say, and what you do are in harmony.” — Mohandas Gandhi

In the beginning, they pull you out by your head.

Nine months in the perfect habitat — warm, dark, quiet — and then you’re yanked out by the part of you most essential to your survival, upside-down, kicking and screaming for your first breath, surrounded by masked ninjas cheering your mother on with an arsenal of shiny tools.

Then you’re poked, prodded, and passed around like an alien at show-and-tell. You cry. They laugh. You cry more. They smile and explain that crying is good. You crawl. You stand. You fall. Nobody asked you.

Welcome to Stop 1 of the Swagger Trail: Is my head on straight?

We never stop talking about our heads

Listen to how much the language leans on this one body part. We fall head over heels for the people who are head and shoulders above the rest. We run around like chickens with our heads cut off until we realize we’re not the head honcho. We try not to lose our heads or get them bitten off. We bury our heads in the sand, bang our heads against the wall, and try to keep our heads up long enough to set the record straight.

That word salad should make your head swim a little. Good. Here’s the point: your head leads your body around. It isn’t responsible for everything that happens to you, but it is responsible for how you respond.

And here’s the first idea of the whole trail, the one everything else hangs on:

You have a head. You are not your head.

Think of your eyes as the windows of a house. They’re openings, not the house. You can’t see much inside through them, especially in the wrong light. The most important rooms — who you really are, what you mean, what’s behind the walls and under the floors — aren’t visible from the window. Getting your head on straight starts with knowing the difference.

Five speeds of the same brain

If the head is a squishy supercomputer on steroids, the way to get it to cooperate starts with understanding its speeds. Brain activity gets sorted into bands by how fast it oscillates:

Delta is the slowest. It runs the stuff you never think about — heartbeat, breathing, digestion, healing — and shows up most in deep sleep and in children. It’s what stills the water enough to see the treasure on the sea floor.

Theta is what I call the hypnotic wave: daydreaming, open-minded, the creator’s playtime zone. Great place to visit. Live there too long, with one foot in dreamland, and it gets hard to stay connected to the physical world.

Alpha is the bridge. It sits right in the middle, linking the deep, unconscious bands with the busy, conscious ones. This is the flow state — athletes, performers, anyone at peak performance with that timeless, fluid feeling. Reaching flow under stress is the goal of this entire stop.

Beta is get-stuff-done mode: math, logic, reading, problem-solving. Most of your day lives here. People who are anxious a lot tend to live here too much.

Gamma is the fastest and most recently studied, tied to learning and pulling new information together into a whole.

Getting your head on straight isn’t about being right about your ideology. It’s being in a state of mind where you can take whatever life throws at you and trust your reaction. Stress is one of the few parts of life where we actually get a say in our response — and the body is how we get a say.

Picture the perfect walk

Before the exercise, try this. Picture someone walking toward you with perfect posture.

Their head is straight, as if a string attached to the crown is lifting it slightly. Their chin is mostly level. Their face is fully in view.

Now notice what you already feel about them. Would you trust them? Follow them? Take orders from them when push comes to shove? Most of us assume, instantly, that this person is smarter, more ethical, just better somehow — better than those of us who hang our heads over devices, vices, and every other hijacker of attention.

They haven’t said a word. That’s Ambulatology.

Exercise 1: Change Your Walk; Change Your Life

This is the first of fifty-three exercises on the trail, and the one I wrote in my notebook first. They’re meant to break your patterns, so they may feel silly. You may feel weird. That’s good. Breaking up the soil is the first step to planting something new.

Find a stretch of floor or sidewalk. Walk a few feet each way:

  1. Chin level. Feel the deliberately awkward integrity of it.
  2. Nod as you walk. Notice the upbeat.
  3. Turn the nod into a bob. Let the upbeat get bigger.
  4. Shake your head side to side. Notice how stilted it feels.
  5. Chin up, watching the sky. Feel the stress melt away. (Or watch out for that hole.)
  6. Look down at the ground. Notice the weight of the world settle on you.
  7. Hold your head as still as possible. Notice the focus.

Seven walks, seven moods, and you didn’t change a single thought to get them. Your head went somewhere, and your body followed. That’s what this whole trail is built on.

A river, not a dam

One last thing to carry with you this week. Consciousness flows like a river. Sticks, leaves, and rubbish float by, sink, and sometimes get stuck. We don’t get much say in which thoughts float and which ones snag. Most of them are chatter, set adrift by whoever or whatever is upstream.

The river doesn’t care what floats in it. So why should you give every passing thought the same importance? The only thing you really control is where you spend your most valuable currency on it: your attention.
