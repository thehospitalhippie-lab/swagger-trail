Ambulatology 101: The "Science" Nobody Taught You in School

How many steps does a person take in a lifetime? Millions? Trillions? Nobody counts until a watch on their wrist starts doing it for them. That’s the strange thing about walking: it’s the most human thing we do, and the thing we think about least.

Last post I told you how I stumbled into Ambulatology. This one is the syllabus — the four ideas you need in your pocket before we hit the first stop on the trail.

Lesson 1: Walking went from do-or-die to a hobby

For most of history, walking was survival. Migration kept people alive. Feet moved the stones that built the pyramids, Stonehenge, and the Easter Island heads. Traders, merchants, and missionaries carried whole cultures across continents as far as their feet could take them.

Then came the combustion engine, and oil became the tar-baby everybody wanted a piece of. People moved toward cities, and we got used to riding. A little over a century ago a car was a curiosity. Now nearly everyone has one.

So walking became recreation. We walk for fresh air, for charity, with our dogs. We literally built a machine for it — the treadmill — and we’re still perfecting it. Doctors now measure how many steps a heart-failure patient can take as a sign of how they’re doing. The thing we used to do to stay alive has become the thing we measure to see if we still are.

And yet it still carries weight nothing else does. A newborn’s first steps. A father walking his daughter down the aisle. A death-row inmate’s last walk down the hall. Walking is our most private act and our most public exposé — the closest we get to being animals without dropping to all fours.

Lesson 2: We talk with our feet more than we know

I mentioned there are 36-plus definitions of “walk.” Listen to how much of the language we actually lean on it. We walk out on a job, walk all over people, walk on air when we’re in love, walk on eggshells around sensitive folks, walk the line like the Man in Black, and take a walk on the wild side with Lou Reed. We get our walking papers. We go the extra mile.

And we don’t just walk. We strut, straggle, stumble, stroll, scuff, shuffle, shamble, amble, saunter, stride, swank, sashay, meander, mosey, gallivant, flounce, float, promenade, dawdle, prance, and parade all over the place.

The steps get their own vocabulary too: a step in the right direction, starting off on the wrong foot, one step forward and two back, being out of step, needing to step up so nobody has to fill your shoes, and always, always watching your step.

Even “putting one foot in front of the other” is a little lie — your feet aren’t actually in line. We bent the phrase to fit the picture we wanted: steady, continuous, going somewhere. That’s the point. The way we talk about walking is the way we talk about living.

Lesson 3: When does a choice become a decision?

As kids, we got to choose. Red sprinkles or blue? Didn’t matter. Wrong choices got you a time-out, and you walked away a little older and wiser.

Then you grow up, and suddenly the IRS and your boss aren’t calling them choices anymore. They’re decisions. The root — de-cise — means to cut one into two. Decisions cut. A bad one can cost you friends, family, or a job. In all walks of life, growing up means owning who you are and every consequence that comes after.

We still make choices as adults every day. The trail ahead is about noticing which ones have quietly become decisions — and which ones your body made before your brain caught up.

Lesson 4: Know thyself, or show thyself?

Tenet nosce — know thyself — shows up in every great culture. Modern America has swapped it for something else: show thyself. We play a dozen roles at once across a dozen feeds in the palm of our hand, swapping masks and hats to signal which tribe we belong to. We beat our chests. Biting someone’s head off online suffices, for now, for most of us.

Here’s the question I want you carrying down the trail instead: “How am I not myself?”

There are three of you. Who you think you are. Who others see. And who you actually are. Self-actualization is getting those three to line up. The version of you that walks on-stage and the version that walks off-stage are both telling the truth about something — and your walk is where they leak into each other.

One honest note: Ambulatology isn’t here to give you a cold reading or a horoscope. There’s a sucker born every minute, as some circus ringleader supposedly said, and I’m not trying to part you from your money. I’m offering a second language — one your body is already fluent in — and a way to start listening to it.

The route ahead

Here’s the whole trail in one breath:

Get it out of your head, past your begrudging shoulders, through the chambers of the heart, up over the fences, down the yellow belly, landing directly in the hips — so you can shake a leg without stifling your stride, toeing the line, going toe-to-toe, tiptoeing toward that one most important thing.

Change your walk; change your life.
